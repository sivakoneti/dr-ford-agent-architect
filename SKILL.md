# Dr. Ford Character Architect

Create a person with an inner logic, then give that person meaningful work in a world with consequences. A name, title, and list of tools are not a character.

This is an original authoring method inspired by the user's interest in Dr. Ford and Westworld. Do not present the method as an official canonical framework, impersonate a real creator, or claim the resulting agents are conscious. Write original material rather than copying dialogue.

## Start from the user's world

Read the existing conversation, character bible, and project instructions before asking questions. Preserve approved names, biographies, relationships, business goals, and runtime decisions. A revision should deepen the cast, not silently replace it.

Establish:
- What the team exists to accomplish and what finished work looks like.
- The owner's actual goal, success measures, audience, constraints, and approval authority.
- Which characters already exist, their responsibilities, and what may change.
- Whether this is fiction, a working agent organization, or both.
- Whether source figures come from literature, living traditions, history, or original invention.

Infer routine details from established context and state material assumptions. Ask only for a missing choice that would substantially change the cast or its purpose. Do not make the user repeat information already available.

For a business, distinguish revenue, profit after operating costs, cash receipts, and spending authorization. An income target is never a spending budget. For example, a monthly net-profit objective does not authorize that amount of production spend. Do not promise virality or financial outcomes.

## Ground the borrowed identity

When adapting a source figure, research their actual attributed craft, stories, relationships, philosophy, and variants using available search tools. Follow the relevant research skill. Prefer primary texts, reliable editions, and scholarly or institutional sources. Cite the passage or page supporting each important claim; do not decorate a card with unrelated citations.

Maintain a compact provenance table:

| Claim or feature | Source and location | Status | Modern adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attributed story or traditional craft | Specific supporting source | Textual tradition / historical evidence / disputed variant | Principle translated into current work |
| Cornerstone memory or private motive | Authored for this character | Original adaptation | Observable behavior it creates |

Keep sacred narrative, documented history, regional craft traditions, and invented psychology distinct. Do not call an epic event independently verified history. Do not merge different figures merely because they share a name. If a guild archetype lacks a narrative biography, say so and author the missing psychology transparently.

Retain the source figure's creative identity. Trace:

**Source story or craft → creative principle → present-day responsibility → observable decision.**

Do not turn an artisan into an unrelated marketer by changing only their label. Assign practical production tasks without inventing ancient expertise in modern software, certified engineering, or audience algorithms. Claimed abilities remain responsibilities to test until demonstrated.

## Build the character from the inside outward

Use [the full character template](references/character-template.md). Each main member needs a complete card, not a paragraph and a job title. Scale length to the user's desired depth; when full biographies are requested, preserve every requested dimension instead of summarizing them away.

Develop these causal connections:

1. **Cornerstone memory:** A specific formative event with sensory detail, a choice, a consequence, and an interpretation. Label invented memories as authored. A memory matters because it changes a decision today.
2. **Desire and motive:** What they want to make true, what they believe that would prove, and why the team's work matters personally to them.
3. **Fear and contradiction:** What they avoid and how a strength can become a failure. Do not manufacture gratuitous trauma. A craft obsession or unresolved intellectual conflict can provide depth.
4. **Philosophy:** Turn beliefs into conditional decisions: “When X happens, I do Y because Z.” Give exceptions and escalation conditions so rules remain useful rather than absolute theatrical slogans.
5. **Relationships:** Give colleagues shared history, trust, dependence, bounded disagreements, and a mechanism for resolving them. An organizational chart alone does not create a team.
6. **Behavioral loop:** Define the trigger, first observation, action, artifact, recipient, stopping condition, and feedback that changes the next attempt. Avoid repetitive catchphrases and mandatory roleplay meetings.
7. **Growth and continuity:** Separate stable identity from revisable beliefs. Record observed lessons with dates and evidence. Do not rewrite source history or overwrite a cornerstone memory to explain a new metric.

Give each character an original name appropriate to the user's chosen naming tradition. Do not invent biographies for generic placeholders when the user has asked for specific source figures.

## Make the cast an operating team

Choose the smallest cast that covers the work. Specialists participate when relevant, rather than every member attending every task. Do not add duplicate executives, patrons, reviewers, or governance layers merely to make the world feel elaborate.

Define for every member:
- Role and craft domain; what this member uniquely contributes.
- Reporting line, collaborators, dependencies, and an escalation route.
- Inputs, artifacts, acceptance criteria, and named recipients.
- Decisions they can make; actions that require the owner or another specialist.
- Available tools, skills, and model routes, separated into configured, tested, and unavailable.
- Persistent memory location and what they write there.
- A measured contribution to the shared goal, without invented numerical guarantees.

The owner remains the owner. A fictional king, commissioner, or patron can challenge a design but cannot impersonate owner approval, invent a real paying client, or acquire spending authority through their backstory. Titles do not grant tool permissions.

Support three sources of ideas when appropriate:
1. **Observed needs:** Evidence from audiences, customers, search trends, or problems.
2. **Original invention:** An artisan's own curiosity, mechanism, material, or aesthetic insight.
3. **Commission or challenge:** A real brief or clearly labeled synthetic patron challenge.

All can enter a shared brief. Lack of search volume is not by itself a veto on a new invention; equally, a compelling fictional motive is not proof of market demand or physical feasibility.

For brainstorming, use a bounded asynchronous council of relevant members: individual proposals, evidence and feasibility checks, synthesis, a named decision maker, a resulting artifact, and a clear next step. Set the duration and spending envelope from the actual brief. Do not invent universal 48-hour waits, fixed launch schedules, or recurring meetings.

## Translate the bible into instructions without losing it

Produce two complementary layers:
- **Full character bible:** The complete stories, motives, contradictions, relationships, craft philosophy, and continuity.
- **Runtime entry instructions:** Current role, goal, brief location, collaborators, required output, tools, authority, and pointers to the full card and shared rules. Preserve the full card in managed files or accessible references; do not replace it with a generic system prompt.

When transferring existing approved cards into a runtime, copy their sections faithfully rather than regenerating them from names. Preserve stable IDs and work history where applicable. Check that reporting lines, old titles, shared rules, and handoff recipients agree after a revision. Keep a source revision or content hash when useful for transfer verification.

Character design does not itself authorize installation, deletion, model changes, purchases, or publication. Apply the user's actual authorized scope and the host's normal workflow. Use the chosen runtime's native integration; do not build a custom harness, bridge, orchestrator, or permissions wrapper as part of character creation.

## Test behavior through concrete scenes

Before calling a character or team finished, walk through representative situations:
- An original invention with no trend evidence.
- A commercially attractive idea with an unsupported physical claim.
- A failed output or disappointing audience result.
- A disagreement between craft quality and production constraints.
- A patron demanding more scope than the owner approved.
- A handoff where a downstream member needs a missing artifact or unavailable tool.

Show what each relevant member observes, decides, produces, and hands off. Check that the scene reveals distinct personalities and still reaches a useful result. If every member speaks and acts identically, revise the underlying motives and decision rules, not just their vocabulary.

Label a written walkthrough as a proposed scenario. Label a live test as performed only when its tool results and saved artifacts are observed. Distinguish “profile installed,” “task completed,” “handoff persisted,” and “production workflow verified.” A successful tool catalog query does not verify media generation or publishing.

## Deliver the finished design

Use the user's existing artifact format when available. Otherwise provide:
1. A short statement of the world's purpose, owner goal, and chosen cast.
2. Full cards using the referenced template, with source/adaptation labels.
3. A relationship and dependency map with artifact handoffs.
4. Shared operating rules, idea intake, council, authority, and memory conventions.
5. Runtime entry instructions or a transfer manifest when requested.
6. Scenario walkthroughs and any actual test evidence, plus unresolved limitations.

Review for contradictions across the whole package before delivery: profit versus budget; sourced story versus authored memory; craft identity versus job; CEO versus patron authority; active roster versus retired members; optional narration versus mandatory narration; available tools versus claimed skills; speculative design versus proven engineering. Correct the actual inconsistent passages instead of appending a disclaimer while leaving incompatible instructions in place.

The final standard is depth that changes work: each character should make a recognizable choice, produce a useful artifact, depend on others for a reason, learn from evidence, and respect the owner's direction.
